1 · Pressure, manometers and hydraulics

PressureCame back

Every problem here is one idea repeated. Pressure changes by ρgh as you go up or down through a liquid, and it is the same at one level inside one connected liquid. Marks go when one term in the walk is dropped, so write the walk as a list of steps from a known point before you add anything up.

Ask Claude about this section
BeforeWTA19 Q32 (three-leg tube with pistons and springs): answered 740, key 18.
NowCTA19 Q24 (manometer with three liquids): answered 3, key 8.
CTA19 Q36 (hydraulic cylinder and piston with oil): answered 187.5, key 37.5.

Same error, until shown otherwise. Same family. The portal figures for Q24 and Q32 are not readable, so I cannot see where those went wrong. Q36 is clear: 187.5 is exactly the force you get with no oil head (6000 N × 25 ÷ 800). The pressure walk was missing the oil column. Check Q24 and Q32 against your paper for the same slip.

The shortcut
  • Pick one level, usually the lowest liquid boundary, and write the pressure there from each side.
  • Going down through a liquid adds ρgh. Going up subtracts it. Use that liquid's own density in each term.
  • A piston adds force ÷ area (its load, a spring force, a push). Two pistons at different heights are at different pressures: add ρgh between them.
  • If pistons move, volume is conserved: A₁x₁ = A₂x₂. The height gap between them then changes by x₁ + x₂.
  • Pascal's law carries a change in pressure. It does not remove the head ρgh.
Worked example · rebuilt from your CTA19 Q36 (its figure is not readable from the portal)

A 600 kg load sits on a piston of area 800 cm². Oil of density 0.75 g/cc joins it to a small piston of area 25 cm² that is 2.0 m higher. Find the force F on the small piston that holds the load. Take g = 10 m/s². Ignore the piston weights and the atmosphere.

Your answer was 187.5 on the real Q36. Correct answer: 37.5 on the real Q36 (150 N on this rebuilt version).

This is the same setup and numbers as the real question, except the height. I changed it so the steps are clear. Your real answer and the key are shown above.

Step 1 · Pressure under the loadLoad weight 600 × 10 = 6000 N on 800 cm² = 0.080 m², so the gauge pressure under it is 6000 ÷ 0.080 = 75 000 Pa.
Step 2 · Move up the oil to the small pistonSame oil, connected, so the pressure drops by ρgh = 750 × 10 × 2.0 = 15 000 Pa over the 2.0 m climb. At the small piston: 75 000 − 15 000 = 60 000 Pa.
Step 3 · Turn pressure into forceF = P × A = 60 000 × 25×10⁻⁴ = 150 N.
Step 4 · Compare with the slipSkip step 2 and you get 75 000 × 25×10⁻⁴ = 187.5 N. That is the number you gave on the real Q36.
Answer: 150 N. Without the oil head it is 187.5 N.
The trap: using Pascal's law as 'pressure is the same everywhere'. Only the change in pressure is passed on. The oil column between the pistons still adds or removes ρgh. The real key, 37.5 N, would need a 60 000 Pa drop, an 8 m oil column. I cannot read the figure, so the 2.0 m here is my own number.

2 · Buoyancy and floating bodies

BuoyancyCame back

A floating body behaves like a spring: push it down by s and the extra buoyant force grows in proportion to s. Work, speed and period questions all follow from that. Marks went when the force was treated as constant, when the tank level was assumed fixed, or when the buoyant force was placed at the wrong point.

Ask Claude about this section
BeforeWTA19 Q33 (work to submerge a floating cylinder): answered 5, key 4.
WTA19 Q34 (non-uniform rod in a liquid, angular acceleration): answered 5, key 110.
NowCTA19 Q21 (cylinder released fully submerged, speed at equilibrium): answered 5, key 3.
CTA19 Q32 (metal cube in mercury, water poured in): answered 20, key 625.
CTA19 Q19 (floating cube): skipped, key 2.

Same family, likely the same error on Q33 and Q21. Q33 and Q21 are the same kind of question: work or speed while the net force changes with depth. Both answers were 5. I cannot see your working, so my read is that the force was treated as constant. Q34 and Q32 are different skills (a torque case and a two-liquid case). Q19 was skipped but takes three lines: removing 200 g lifts the cube 2 cm, so side² × 2 cm = 200 cm³, side = 10 cm = 5x, x = 2.

The shortcut
  • Float first: the fraction submerged is ρ_body / ρ_liquid. Equilibrium is where the net force is zero.
  • Displace by s and the net force is ρ_liq g A s, linear in s. Work or speed comes from the area under a triangle, ½ k s². Never use a constant force.
  • In a tank of area A_tank, the level moves. The extra submerged depth is s × A_tank / (A_tank − A), so the effective spring is stiffer than in a wide tank.
  • For a tilted rod, buoyancy acts at the middle of the submerged part. Take torques about the hinge. Weight and buoyancy have different arms.
  • Floating ice that melts adds m/ρ_water of water. Compare that with the volume it displaced. With two liquids, the ice sits across the interface.
Worked example · the question you got wrong (CTA19 Q21)

A cylinder of density ρ/4 (ρ is the liquid's density) and length l = 40 cm is released from rest just fully submerged in a wide tank. Find its speed, in m/s, the first time it passes its equilibrium position. Take g = 10 m/s².

Your answer was 5. Correct answer: 3.

Your 5 matches the constant-force slip. I cannot see your working, so that is my best guess.

Step 1 · Where it floatsDensity ratio 1/4, so at rest it floats with l/4 = 10 cm under water. Released with its top at the surface, it rises 3l/4 = 30 cm before it settles (wide tank, level fixed).
Step 2 · Net force at the startB − W = ρVg − ρVg/4 = ¾ ρ g A l. It points up. At equilibrium it is zero.
Step 3 · Net force as it risesAfter rising y, less of it is under water: F(y) = ρ g A (3l/4 − y). It falls in a straight line from ¾ρgAl to zero over 30 cm. That is the graph below.
Step 4 · Work to kinetic energy½mv² = ½ × (¾ρgAl) × (3l/4) = (9/32) ρ g A l². With m = (ρ/4) A l: v² = (9/4) g l.
Step 5 · Put in numbersv² = 2.25 × 10 × 0.40 = 9, so v = 3 m/s. The constant-force slip gives v² = 2 × 30 × 0.40 = 24, about 4.9, which rounds to the 5 you gave.
Answer: 3 m/s
The trap: treating the net force as the constant starting value 3g × m. The buoyant force drops as the cylinder comes out of the water, so only the triangle under the line counts. The question wording here is how the portal recorded it. The key of 3 m/s matches this reading.

3 · Accelerating and rotating frames

Pseudo-forceNew

When a container accelerates, the water, the ball and the buoyant force all feel a new effective gravity. Replace g by g′ in every formula and the problem is an ordinary one again. Marks went when g′ was used in one place and plain g in another.

Ask Claude about this section

Not yet a pattern: this appeared only in WTA19 (Q35 wrong, Q26 and Q28 partial). It is a small set of rules, so it gets two practice problems.

The shortcut
  • Lift or tank accelerating up at a: g′ = g + a. Down at a: g′ = g − a. Horizontal at a: g′ = √(g² + a²), tilted back from the vertical by tan θ = a/g.
  • Use g′ everywhere: weight m g′, buoyancy ρ_liq V g′, and pressure change ρ g′ h. The free surface sits at right angles to g′.
  • A floating body keeps the same submerged fraction, because weight and buoyancy both scale with g′.
  • Rotating liquid (use Newton's second law in the ground frame): the pressure gradient supplies the centripetal force, dp/dr = ρω²r, and dp/dz = −ρg. The free surface is z = ω²r²/(2g) above its lowest point.
  • The paraboloid hollow holds half the volume of its enclosing cylinder, so the average height stays at the rest depth. Use that to find where the surface starts.
Worked example · the question you got wrong (WTA19 Q35)

A sphere of mass 2 kg and specific gravity 0.5 is tied to the bottom of a tank of water. The tank accelerates upward at 2 m/s². The string tension is 8n newtons. Find n. Take g = 10 m/s².

Your answer was 3.5. Correct answer: 3.

Step 1 · Switch to the tank frameThe tank accelerates up at 2 m/s², so everything inside feels g′ = g + a = 12 m/s² instead of g. Weight and buoyancy both use it.
Step 2 · Volume of the sphereSpecific gravity 0.5 gives density 500 kg/m³, so V = 2 ÷ 500 = 4×10⁻³ m³.
Step 3 · BuoyancyB = ρ_w V g′ = 1000 × 0.004 × 12 = 48 N, upward.
Step 4 · Weightm g′ = 2 × 12 = 24 N, downward. The sphere floats up, so the string pulls it down.
Step 5 · TensionT = 48 − 24 = 24 N = 8n, so n = 3. One line: T = (ρ_w − ρ_s) V g′ = 500 × 0.004 × 12 = 24 N.
Answer: n = 3
The trap: using g′ = 12 for the buoyant force but plain g = 10 for the weight. That gives 48 − 20 = 28 N, which is 8 × 3.5, exactly the answer you gave. I cannot see your working, so this is my best read.

4 · SHM and effective inertia

SHMCame back

A small-oscillation problem is two measurements: how stiff (U″ at equilibrium) and how heavy (the effective inertia from the kinetic energy). The slips come from the second one: a spinning part, a second block on a string, or a coordinate that is not a plain position. Write the kinetic energy in one coordinate first and read the effective inertia off it.

Ask Claude about this section
BeforeCTA17 Q31 (ball on a table with a hanging block, small radial oscillation): answered 4, key 1.73.
CTA17 Q32 (ring on a rod, T₂/T₁): answered 4, key 1.41.
NowCTA19 Q26 (spring-coupled pistons) and Q29 (bead on a rotating rod): marked one option each and got partial marks.

Different error, partial progress. Both CTA17 answers were 4, and I cannot tell from the portal whether the energy setup was started. In CTA19 you found one correct option on each question. The setup seems to be working better now. The gap is testing every other option after the first one.

The shortcut
  • ω² = U″(equilibrium) / m_eff, where KE = ½ m_eff q̇². Write KE with the constraint in it before you differentiate anything.
  • A part that spins adds to m_eff. A hoop rolling inside a track of radius R gives 2m(R−r)². A solid disk gives (3/2)m(R−r)². Sliding with no spin gives m(R−r)².
  • Two masses joined by a string move together, so add them: table ball plus hanging block gives (M + m). For radial oscillation, keep angular momentum fixed first, then d/dr (1/r³) brings a factor 3.
  • Coulomb (sliding) friction does not change the period. Each half-cycle loses 2μmg/k of amplitude, and the block stops once |kx| ≤ μmg. Viscous damping does lengthen the period.
  • Multi-correct: after you find one true option, test every other option directly. Often the next one is a consequence of the first.
Worked example · the question you got wrong (CTA17 Q32)

A ring of radius R hangs on a fixed horizontal rod of radius r₀ and swings with small amplitude, its plane at right angles to the rod. T₁ is the period when the rod is smooth. T₂ is the period when the ring rolls on the rod without slipping. Find T₂/T₁.

Your answer was 4. Correct answer: 1.41 (√2).

Step 1 · Choose the coordinate and the potentialLet θ be the angle of line OC from the vertical. Both cases share U = −mg(R − r₀) cos θ, so U″ = mg(R − r₀).
Step 2 · Smooth rod: the ring does not spinCentre speed v = (R − r₀) θ̇. KE = ½ m (R − r₀)² θ̇², so m_eff = m(R − r₀)² and T₁ = 2π √((R − r₀)/g).
Step 3 · Rolling: find the spinThe contact point P is at rest, so v = ω_spin R. With v = (R − r₀) θ̇: ω_spin = (R − r₀) θ̇ / R. It is not θ̇.
Step 4 · Rolling: kinetic energyKE = ½mv² + ½(mR²)ω_spin² = ½m(R−r₀)²θ̇² + ½m(R−r₀)²θ̇² = m(R − r₀)² θ̇². So m_eff = 2m(R − r₀)² and T₂ = 2π √(2(R − r₀)/g).
Step 5 · RatioT₂ / T₁ = √2 = 1.41.
Answer: T₂/T₁ = √2 = 1.41
The trap: using θ̇ as the spin rate, or forgetting the spin energy. The potential is the same in both cases, so the whole difference sits in the effective inertia. The wording follows the portal's record: 'friction versus frictionless', and the key √2 fits T₂ over T₁ this way.

A closed tank of gas is connected to a U-tube manometer. The gas presses on a 30 cm column of water that rests on mercury in the left limb. The mercury surface in the right limb is 5 cm higher than in the left. Above it is a 40 cm column of oil (specific gravity 0.8), open to the atmosphere. Mercury has specific gravity 13.6 and g = 10 m/s². Find the gauge pressure of the gas.

Pick one option.

Answer: B · 7.0 kPa
Take the level of the left mercury surface. Left: p_gas + ρ_w g(0.30) = p_gas + 3000. Right (the mercury is one connected liquid, so this level is the same): p_atm + ρ_oil g(0.40) + ρ_Hg g(0.05) = p_atm + 3200 + 6800. So p_gas − p_atm = 10 000 − 3000 = 7000 Pa.
  • A: left out the 5 cm of mercury: 3200 − 3000 = 200 Pa
  • C: left out the water column: 3200 + 6800 = 10 000 Pa
  • D: added the water column instead of subtracting it: 3200 + 6800 + 3000 = 13 000 Pa

3Single-correctPistons and springL4

Two vertical cylinders of area 100 cm² (left) and 50 cm² (right) are joined at the bottom and hold water. Each is closed by a light, frictionless piston resting on the water, and the water levels start equal. The right piston is held down by a spring (k = 500 N/m, at its natural length when the levels are equal) fixed to the ceiling above it. A 2.3 kg mass is placed on the left piston. Ignore the atmosphere, which acts equally on both. How far does the left piston go down in equilibrium?

Pick one option.

Answer: B · 1.0 cm
Water volume is conserved: 100 x₁ = 50 x₂, so x₂ = 2x₁. The pistons end x₁ + x₂ = 3x₁ apart in height. Pressure under the left piston: 23 N ÷ 0.0100 = 2300 Pa. Under the right piston: k x₂ / A₂ = 500 × 2x₁ ÷ 0.0050 = 200 000 x₁, and it must equal 2300 − 1000 × 10 × 3x₁. So 2300 = 230 000 x₁, x₁ = 0.010 m.
  • A: took x₂ = x₁ (ignored the area ratio): 2300 = 120 000 x₁, 1.9 cm
  • C: set the spring force equal to the weight, kx₂ = Mg, so x₂ = 4.6 cm and x₁ = 2.3 cm
  • D: left out the water-height difference between the pistons: 2300 = 200 000 x₁, 1.15 cm

9Single-correctRolling diskL4

A uniform solid disk of mass m and radius r rolls without slipping, in a vertical plane, inside a fixed circular track of radius R (R is larger than r). It makes small oscillations about the lowest point. What is the period?

Pick one option.

Answer: C · 2π√(3(R − r)/(2g))
Centre speed v = (R − r) θ̇ and spin ω = v/r. KE = ½mv² + ½(½mr²)(v/r)² = ¾ m (R − r)² θ̇², so m_eff = (3/2) m (R − r)². U = mg(R − r)(1 − cos θ) gives U″ = mg(R − r). So ω² = 2g / (3(R − r)) and T = 2π√(3(R − r)/(2g)).
